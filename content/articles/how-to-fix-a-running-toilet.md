A running toilet is one of the most wasteful household problems there is - a slow, constant hiss or an occasional refill that never quite stops can send hundreds of litres of water through the fixture every day, quietly, while you barely notice. It is also, for the most part, one of the easier plumbing repairs: in many toilets the cause is a worn flapper, an out-of-adjustment fill valve, or a water level set too high, all of which are accessible inside the tank and often fixable with cheap parts and a few minutes.

This guide walks through diagnosing the running toilet, then fixing the most common causes one at a time: the flapper, the fill valve, the water level and overflow tube, the refill tube, and the tank-to-bowl gasket where relevant. It also covers the dye test, the silence-versus-noise distinction, and when a running toilet points to a bigger problem or needs a part you cannot easily source. The goal is to stop the water waste, stop the noise, and get the toilet to behave again with the least work that will do it.

You need a screwdriver, an adjustable wrench, a sponge or rag, a bucket or bowl, and, for most repairs, a replacement flapper and possibly a new fill valve matched to the toilet. Parts are usually inexpensive and widely available; the main challenge is matching the part to the toilet, since toilets are not all identical. A few minutes of diagnosis before buying parts saves the wrong trip.

## Why a Toilet Runs

A toilet tank is a simple device: water fills the tank until a float or sensor tells the fill valve to shut off; when you flush, the flapper lifts and lets tank water rush into the bowl; when the tank empties, the flapper falls back and the fill valve refills the tank to its set level.

A running toilet usually means one of these things is off:

- The flapper is not sealing the flush valve, so water leaks from the tank into the bowl and the fill valve keeps topping it off.
- The fill valve does not shut off fully, so the tank keeps filling and water spills into the overflow tube, or the valve shuts off only partly and then leaks back on.
- The water level is set too high and spills into the overflow tube.
- The refill tube is misrouted, sending water into the overflow in a way that keeps things running or re-triggers the fill.
- A less common cause is a crack in the overflow tube, a bad tank-to-bowl gasket, or a problem with the flush valve seat.

The first two - flapper leak and fill valve problem - account for most running toilets by far.

## Diagnose Before You Repair

Diagnosis saves parts and time. Before taking anything apart, spend a minute listening and looking.

### Listen first

A running toilet often sounds like one of these:

- A steady hiss while the tank is full - often the fill valve not shutting fully.
- A intermittent trickle or periodic refill when no one has used the toilet - often a slow flapper leak.
- A loud, obvious running sound - more likely a flapper leak or a water level too high.

A toilet that runs only right after a flush may just have a long fill time or a misadjusted valve; a toilet that runs or trickles through the day is usually leaking from tank to bowl.

### Look inside the tank

Take the lid off carefully and set it somewhere safe - toilet lids are heavy, awkward, and easy to crack. Look while the tank is still:

- Is water flowing into the overflow tube?
- Is the water level above the top of the overflow tube?
- Does the flapper look warped, cracked, mineral-crusted, or out of position?
- Is the chain between the flush handle and the flapper too tight or too loose?
- Is the refill tube pointing into the overflow tube in the wrong way?

The overflow tube is the vertical tube in the middle of the tank. If water is running into it when the tank is full, the water level is too high or the fill valve is overfilling; if water is leaking past the flapper into the bowl, the tank water level will slowly drop and the fill valve will cycle back on to refill it.

### The dye test

To confirm a flapper or tank-to-bowl leak, use the dye test:

1. Make sure the toilet has not been flushed recently and the tank is full.
2. Put a few drops of food colouring - dark works best, such as blue or red - into the tank water.
3. Wait 15 to 30 minutes without flushing.
4. Look in the bowl. If coloured water appears in the bowl, water is leaking from the tank into the bowl, most often past the flapper.

If the bowl stays clear, the leak may be the fill valve overfilling into the overflow, not a flapper leak. That distinction matters, because the fixes are different.

### Is the leak tank-to-bowl or fill valve-overflow?

- Colour appears in the bowl - likely flapper or tank gasket leak.
- Water running into the overflow tube, or the water level above the overflow - likely fill valve or water level issue.
- Both - a combination; fix the flapper first, then the fill valve and water level.

Once you know which, the repair is much clearer.

## Fix 1: The Flapper

The flapper is the washer-like or shaped rubber piece that sits over the flush valve opening at the bottom of the tank and drops to seal it after a flush.

### When the flapper is the cause

Common signs:

- Intermittent running or trickling.
- Colour appears in the bowl in the dye test.
- The flapper looks warped, cracked, stiff, or mineral-crusted.
- The flapper is not dropping fully into place, or not seating flat on the valve seat.

### Service the flapper first

Often the flapper does not need replacing; it needs cleaning or a minor adjustment.

1. Turn off the water and flush to empty the tank.
2. Sponge out remaining water if needed.
3. Check the flapper seat - the rim the flapper rests on. Mineral deposits or grit on the seat can prevent a seal. Clean the seat gently with a cloth or sponge; do not scratch it.
4. Clean the flapper itself with water. If it is covered in scale or deposits, cleaning can restore the seal.
5. Check the chain. If the chain is too tight, it can hold the flapper slightly open; if too loose, it may not lift the flapper fully or may get caught. Adjust so the flapper can seat fully but the chain has only a little slack.
6. Check that the flapper is seated correctly and the ears or attachment are properly on the overflow or posts.
7. Turn the water back on, let the tank fill, and test.

If cleaning and adjustment do not stop the leak, replace the flapper.

### Replacing the flapper

Most flappers are inexpensive and come in a few shapes and attachment styles. Match the replacement to the toilet as closely as possible.

1. Turn off the water and empty the tank.
2. Remove the old flapper by unhooking it from the posts or overflow, and from the chain.
3. Note how the old flapper attaches and what shape it is - photographs help.
4. Install the new flapper in the same way.
5. Reconnect the chain with the proper slack.
6. Turn the water on, test the flush, and check for a clean seal with the dye test if you want to confirm.

A mismatched flapper can leak even when new, so if the new one does not seal, check the attachment style and the valve seat again. In rare cases the flush valve itself is the wrong size or shape for the available flappers, and you may need a universal kit or a different replacement.

### How the flapper can fail

- Rubber warps or stiffens over time, especially in hot water or with older chlorinated or treated water.
- Mineral deposits build up on the seat and flapper.
- The chain misadjusts over time.
- The flapper material degrades and no longer forms a seal.

Flappers are wear parts; if a toilet is old and the flapper has failed once, another leak is not surprising a year or two later.

## Fix 2: The Fill Valve

The fill valve controls water coming into the tank. When it fails, the tank either overfills or never fully shuts off.

### Signs the fill valve is the problem

- A steady hissing after the tank is full.
- The water level rising above the overflow tube.
- The valve never fully shutting off, or shutting off and then slowly leaking back on.
- An old fill valve that has worn out.

### Clean the fill valve

Some fill valves can be cleaned by removing the cap or top and rinsing out sediment or debris that is keeping the valve from seating. If your valve is of a type that allows this and the problem is mild, a cleaning may restore it. Follow the valve's design; not all can be opened the same way.

If cleaning does not help, replace the valve. Fill valves are inexpensive and replacement is straightforward on most toilets.

### Replacing the fill valve

A replacement fill valve is usually sized to standard toilets, but check compatibility before buying, especially for older or non-standard models.

1. Turn off the water and empty the tank.
2. Disconnect the supply line from the bottom of the fill valve. Have a small container ready for the water left in the line and tank.
3. Remove the old fill valve - usually by unscrewing it from the tank base.
4. Install the new fill valve, adjusting its height to suit the tank as directed by the instructions.
5. Reconnect the supply line.
6. Set the float or adjustment according to the replacement's instructions.
7. Turn the water on, fill the tank, and adjust the water level so it stops below the top of the overflow tube.
8. Flush and check.

Replacing the fill valve often solves the running-toilet problem more permanently than fiddling with an old one, especially on older fixtures.

## Fix 3: The Water Level and Overflow Tube

The overflow tube is there as a safety: if the fill valve fails to shut off, the overflow tube carries excess water into the bowl rather than letting the tank overflow over the top of the tank. But if the water level is set too high, the overflow tube becomes a constant leak path.

### Set the water level correctly

The water level should sit below the top of the overflow tube, usually marked on the overflow tube or the fill valve. If the water is level with or above the top, water is constantly going into the bowl.

- For a float-arm fill valve, adjust the float so the water stops at the proper level. On some, there is a screw or a bend in the arm.
- For a float-cup or cylinder fill valve, adjust the clip or screw that sets the float height.

After adjusting, flush and let the tank refill to confirm the water stops at the right point.

### Check the refill tube

The refill tube is a small tube that sends some water into the bowl after a flush to restore the bowl water level. It should be routed into the overflow tube by a clip or rearranging, but it should not be positioned so that water keeps running continuously into the overflow in a way that drives the fill valve.

- Make sure the refill tube is not shoved down into the overflow tube in a manner that creates a siphon or constant flow.
- Make sure it is clipped above the overflow opening in the way the manufacturer intended.

A misrouted refill tube can cause a running or refilling toilet in subtle ways; a quick look at it is worth it.

## Fix 4: The Tank-to-Bowl Gasket

Less commonly, a running or leaking toilet has a leak between the tank and the bowl through the gasket or bolts. Signs can include water on the floor around the base, a leak between tank and bowl, or a toilet that runs even though the flapper and fill valve seem fine.

This is a larger repair than a flapper or fill valve, because it involves separating the tank from the bowl. If you see water on the floor, first confirm the source - a sweating tank or a supply line leak can look similar. If the leak is clearly between tank and bowl, replacing the tank-to-bowl gasket and possibly the bolts is the repair.

If you are not comfortable with this, or if the bolts are corroded, it is reasonable to call a plumber; tank-to-bowl leaks are more involved and easier to do wrong than a flapper swap.

## Other Causes Worth Checking

- **A faulty flush valve seat.** If the seat is corroded or damaged, a new flapper may not seal well. A seat-dressing tool exists for some problems, but often the practical fix is a new flapper first and, if that fails, a new flush valve or a call to a plumber.
- **A cracked overflow tube.** Rare, but possible; if the overflow tube is cracked above the water line, it may still leak or run in a way that is hard to diagnose.
- **A supply-line or shut-off valve problem.** A leaking shut-off valve or a supply line that drips or weeps can mimic or accompany a running toilet. Check the valve and supply line too.
- **A phantom refill.** A toilet that refills briefly and quietly every so often is often a small flapper leak; the dye test helps confirm it.

## Step-by-Step: A Practical Repair Order

For most running toilets, the efficient order is:

1. Shut off the water and empty the tank.
2. Listen and look: determine whether the leak is flapper-related or overflow/fill-valve-related.
3. Do the dye test if the source is unclear.
4. Clean the flapper and the valve seat, adjust the chain, and test.
5. If the flapper still leaks, replace the flapper.
6. If the problem is the fill valve or water level, adjust or replace the fill valve and set the water level below the overflow tube.
7. Check the refill tube routing.
8. Turn the water on, flush, and confirm the toilet stops running and refills normally.

This order avoids replacing parts that did not need replacing, and it catches the most common causes first.

## When to Call a Plumber

Call a plumber when:

- The shut-off valve will not shut off and you cannot safely work on the toilet.
- The toilet still runs after replacing the flapper and fill valve and adjusting the water level.
- There is a leak between tank and bowl and you are not comfortable separating them.
- The toilet is old and parts are not matching well, or the flush valve needs replacing.
- There is water on the floor and you cannot identify the source.
- The home has a more complex plumbing setup, or you suspect a bigger problem.

A plumber can also be the right call when the repair is small but the risk of making it worse - cracked porcelain, stripped bolts, a valve that will not shut - feels too high. Porcelain cracks easily if dropped or stressed; a careful hand is worth more than a rushed fix.

## Saving Water and Money

A running toilet is a quiet water bill and a quiet waste. Stopping it often costs only a flapper, a fill valve, or a few minutes of adjustment. The dye test and the careful look inside the tank are the two most useful minutes you can spend; they tell you which repair will actually help.

If your water meter is accessible, you can do a simple check for a slow leak: make sure no water is being used in the house, then watch the meter for movement over a period. A running toilet often shows up there even when you cannot hear it.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating every running toilet as a flapper problem without checking the fill valve and water level.
- Replacing a flapper with the wrong type and wondering why it still leaks.
- Leaving the chain too tight, so the flapper cannot seat properly.
- Setting the water level too high so the overflow tube keeps leaking.
- Forgetting to turn off the water before working inside the tank.
- Using too much force on porcelain, which can crack it.
- Ignoring a shut-off valve that leaks or does not shut off fully.

## The Bottom Line

Fix a running toilet by first identifying the cause - usually a flapper that will not seal or a fill valve or water level that keeps the tank overfilling - then repairing the specific part. Start with the simple, cheap fixes: clean and service the flapper, replace the flapper, and check the water level and overflow. If the fill valve is the issue, replace it. A running toilet is a small repair that stops a large waste, and most of them are solved with parts that cost little and a careful diagnosis before you buy anything.
