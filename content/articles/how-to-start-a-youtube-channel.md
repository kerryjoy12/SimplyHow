Starting a YouTube channel is easy in the mechanical sense - clicking "create channel" takes a minute - and hard in the way that matters: making videos strangers will watch to the end. The creators who last are not the ones with the best cameras; they are the ones who pick a clear topic, publish on a schedule, and improve visibly from video to video.

This guide covers the whole path in order: choosing a niche you will not abandon, naming and setting up the channel, filming with what you already own, editing efficiently, writing titles and thumbnails that earn clicks, and the publishing habits that compound into growth. We finish with the first-fifty-videos mindset that gets every channel through the awkward start.

You need a phone or computer, a weekend to set up, and about 40 minutes a week to film and publish once you are in rhythm. No prior experience, no agency, no expensive gear.

## Step 1: Pick a Niche You Can Sustain

A niche is simply the intersection of three questions:

1. **What do I know or enjoy enough to discuss for 50 videos?**
2. **What do people search for or watch repeatedly?**
3. **What is narrow enough that a viewer knows what your channel is about after one visit?**

Specific beats broad every time. "Cooking" is a category; "30-minute weeknight dinners for small kitchens" is a channel. "Tech" is a category; "second-hand laptops and how to fix them" is a channel. Narrowness feels limiting and actually liberates you: you always know what your next video is.

Validate the niche by searching YouTube for the topic and looking at channels in the range of 5,000-100,000 subscribers with recent videos. If they get consistent views, demand exists. Note their most popular videos by sorting by "Popular" - those titles are your research, not your script.

## Step 2: Name the Channel

Good channel names are short, sayable and available. Check that the exact name is free as a YouTube handle (the @name), as a channel name, and ideally as a social handle elsewhere so your branding does not fragment.

Ways that work:

- **Descriptive:** "Home Gym on a Budget" - instantly clear
- **Personal:** your own name - flexible if topics evolve, best if you are the brand
- **Invented word:** memorable once known, but you must teach people what it means

Avoid numbers, hyphens, and names tied to a trend that will date. You can change the channel name and handle later, but each change confuses existing viewers slightly - choose once and move on.

## Step 3: Set Up the Channel Properly

A complete channel page converts curious visitors into subscribers. Set up:

1. **Profile picture** - a clear face or simple logo at 800×800 px, readable at thumbnail size.
2. **Banner** - 2048×1152 px, telling visitors what they get and when you post ("Weekly reviews of used laptops").
3. **Channel description** - first two lines matter most: who it is for, what they learn, and the upload schedule. Keywords belong here naturally.
4. **Links** - website and the social profiles you will actually maintain.
5. **Sections and playlists** - organize your first videos into playlists by topic; playlists increase session watch time and make your channel look established.
6. **About details** - contact email for business enquiries (a dedicated address, not your personal one).
7. **Advanced settings** - set the channel's audience correctly ("made for kids" only if it genuinely is; getting this wrong has legal consequences).

Finish the visual identity before the first upload: consistent colors and a recognizable thumbnail style are part of what makes a channel look trustworthy.

## Step 4: Plan Your First 10 Videos

Do not launch with one idea and improvise afterwards. Write down 10 video topics before filming anything, using your "Popular" research from step 1 plus questions people actually ask you.

Structure them in three groups:

- **Search-driven videos** answering specific questions ("How to...", "Why does...", comparisons) - these bring new viewers for months.
- **Starter videos** establishing who you are - a channel trailer, your story, what to expect.
- **Pillar videos** your channel is built around - the topics you could re-cover a year from now with new information.

A healthy early mix is roughly 7 search-driven, 2 starter, 1 pillar. Write each title before filming; a video without a title you are excited about is a video without an audience.

For planning discipline, use the same weekly system described in our [weekly planning guide](/posts/how-to-plan-your-week/) - one filming block beats scattered attempts every time.

## Step 5: Film with What You Own

Your phone records excellent video. What matters more than resolution is the three things viewers notice instantly:

**Audio.** Bad audio loses viewers faster than bad video. Film in a soft-furnished room (rugs, curtains and sofas absorb echo), speak toward a wall rather than out a window, and consider a cheap lavalier mic ($15-30) that plugs into the phone. Watch any popular creator's video muted, then unmuted - you will feel why this matters.

**Light.** Face a window during daylight and you have a free, flattering key light. At night, put a lamp slightly above and to the side of your face; never shoot with a window behind you, which turns you into a silhouette.

**Stability.** A $20 phone tripod eliminates the handheld wobble that reads as amateur. If nothing else, prop the phone against a stack of books - anything to stop movement.

Frame yourself slightly off-centre with headroom, shoot in landscape (unless making Shorts), and record at the highest resolution your phone offers so you can crop in while editing.

## Step 6: Structure the Video Itself

Viewers decide within seconds whether to stay. Structure accordingly:

1. **The hook (first 15-30 seconds):** state the problem and the payoff - "Your laptop is slow because of nine startup programs - I'll show you how to fix all of them in ten minutes."
2. **Context (short):** why this matters, what you will cover, what they need.
3. **The body:** one point per segment, each with a visual change - cut to a demo, a screen recording, a different angle.
4. **Recap:** thirty seconds summarising the steps.
5. **Call to action:** one clear next step - subscribe for the follow-up, watch a related video, comment with their result.

Keep it as short as the content allows. Five tight minutes beat fifteen padded ones; retention curves drop when viewers sense filler.

## Step 7: Edit Efficiently

Editing is where beginners lose weeks. Keep it simple and consistent:

**Choose one editor and learn it.** CapCut (free, desktop and mobile), DaVinci Resolve (free, professional), iMovie (free on Mac) and Premiere Pro (paid) all work. Free tools are entirely sufficient for your first hundred videos.

**A reliable workflow:**

1. Rough cut: remove mistakes, silences and false starts.
2. Arrange segments in the order you planned.
3. Add text overlays for key points - many viewers watch without sound initially.
4. Cut dead air aggressively; pauses feel twice as long on screen.
5. Add music at low volume (use YouTube's Audio Library to stay copyright-safe).
6. Export at 1080p, H.264, and check it on a phone before uploading.

**Speed hacks:** create a template project with your intro, lower-thirds and export settings; use keyboard shortcuts until cutting feels automatic; batch three or four videos in one editing session. Our [phone video editing guide](/posts/how-to-edit-videos-on-your-phone/) covers doing all of this on a phone if you do not own a computer.

## Step 8: Make Thumbnails and Titles That Earn the Click

Your video is competing for a slot in someone's feed against creators with bigger budgets. Two elements decide whether you get the impression:

**Thumbnails.** One clear subject, high contrast, maximum three or four words of text, a face with an expressive reaction when relevant, and consistent branding colors. Make it legible at 160 pixels wide - shrink your draft to phone size and check. Never let YouTube auto-pick a random frame if you can help it.

**Titles.** Specific, curiosity-opening, and honest - between about 40 and 70 characters so nothing critical truncates. "My laptop was slow - 15 fixes that worked" beats "Laptop tips video". Put the main keyword where it still reads naturally, and make sure title and thumbnail say *different* things so together they carry more than either alone.

Write the title and thumbnail **before** filming whenever possible; they focus the video's promise.

## Step 9: Publish with SEO Basics

YouTube is a search engine as much as a feed. On every upload:

- **Description:** first two lines contain the hook and main keyword (they show in search), then a written summary with timestamps, then links and your standard outro text.
- **Tags:** a handful of relevant phrases - helpful for context, not a magic trick.
- **Playlist:** add the video to a relevant playlist so it sits in a session.
- **Cards and end screens:** link the two or three videos most related to this one; end screens lift session time meaningfully.
- **Captions:** auto-captions are decent - review and fix them; accurate captions help accessibility and search.
- **Category:** set it correctly (Howto & Style, Education, and so on).

Publish at a consistent time on a consistent day. Your audience learns when to expect you, and YouTube's systems treat reliable publishing as a positive signal.

## Step 10: Build a Sustainable Publishing Habit

Growth comes from consistency compounded over months, so design a routine you can keep on a bad week:

- **One concept per week** from your 10-video list - never film without a decided title.
- **Batch filming:** one session produces two or three videos.
- **Fixed editing slot:** same day weekly, protected like an appointment.
- **Monthly review:** which video performed best, and what does that say about next month's topics?

Put the slots in your calendar the way our [planning guide](/posts/how-to-plan-your-week/) describes - commitments without time slots are wishes.

## Growing Past the First 50 Videos

The first 50 videos are your apprenticeship: skills improve, the algorithm learns who watches you, and you discover which of your topics has pull. During this phase:

- **Study retention graphs** in YouTube Studio - the point where viewers leave tells you exactly what to fix.
- **Double down on winners:** if one topic outperforms, make three variations.
- **Reply to every early comment** - early viewers become your core audience and they read replies.
- **Collaborate** with similarly sized channels in your niche - a shared video borrows an audience honestly.
- **Shorts as trailers:** short clips that point to your long-form videos can introduce you to new viewers cheaply; treat them as marketing, not the main event.

Avoid the metrics trap: compare this month to last month, not to channels five years ahead of you. Every large creator has an early library they are embarrassed by.

## Troubleshooting

**No views after 10 videos.** Normal. Check three things: are titles solving a searched problem, do thumbnails read at small sizes, and do videos deliver on the title's promise within the first minute? Fix those before blaming the algorithm.

**Videos take too long to make.** Simplify the format: fewer camera angles, a fixed template, tighter scripts. Consistency at a smaller scale beats irregular perfection.

**Burnout or running out of ideas.** Return to your niche research: sort similar channels by Popular, read your comments, and answer the questions you have been asked personally. Idea drought usually means the niche is too broad, not too narrow.

**Negative comments shake you.** Read criticism for the one useful fact it might contain, then move on. Spam and abuse get deleted without response - you owe them nothing.

**The channel is growing slowly but steadily - should you quit?** If videos improve month over month and a small audience genuinely engages, you are on the path most successful creators walked. Judge the trend, not the absolute number.

## The Bottom Line

Starting a YouTube channel is a sequence of ordinary, doable decisions: a narrow niche you can sustain, a clear identity, a complete channel page, ten planned videos, phone footage with good audio and light, tight editing, honest titles and thumbnails, and a weekly rhythm you can keep for a year. Gear, trends and algorithm tricks come later - and matter far less. Publish, learn, repeat: your first video will be rough, your tenth will be better, and your fiftieth will be good enough that strangers subscribe.
