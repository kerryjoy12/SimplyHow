faqs:
  - q: What is the best place to put a Wi-Fi router?
    a: Centred, elevated, and out in the open - ideally central to the house and high on a shelf, not inside a cabinet or behind a TV. Walls, floors, metal, water and appliances all block signal, so the ideal spot is plainly visible and roughly equidistant from the furthest devices rather than tucked in a corner.
  - q: Is a Wi-Fi extender the same as a mesh network?
    a: No. A Wi-Fi extender repeats the signal from one router, often on a different channel, and usually cuts speed roughly in half for devices on the extended band. A mesh system uses multiple nodes that talk to each other as one network, giving you the same name, smooth hand-offs between rooms and better seamless coverage - the difference matters most in larger homes.
  - q: Will changing the Wi-Fi channel help?
    a: Possibly. If neighbouring Wi-Fi networks are on the same channel as yours, the air gets crowded and speeds drop, especially on 2.4 GHz. Scanning the air and choosing a less-used channel can help, but the gains are usually modest compared with better placement and band selection. Modern routers with automatic channel selection often handle this adequately.
  - q: Should I use 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz?
    a: 5 GHz is faster and less congested over distance, but travels less far and through fewer obstacles; 2.4 GHz reaches further and penetrates walls better but is often crowded and slower. Use 5 GHz for devices near the router and on video calls and gaming, 2.4 GHz for devices at range; many modern routers let you pick per-device, and the best setup lets you use whichever band fits each room.
  - q: Do Wi-Fi boosters actually work?
    a: They repeat the signal and can help in a specific dead zone, but they do not create a new source of strong signal - they extend a weak one. A single well-placed extend point can help a room at the fringe of your range for casual use; if your whole house needs help, a mesh system or a relocating router are the better answer.

Half the house loads one website instantly and the other half cannot even load a photo. Wi-Fi like that is not just annoying - it turns simple tasks, and especially video calls and streaming in the back rooms, into a gamble. The causes are usually not mysterious: wall thickness, distance, interference from appliances, a badly placed router, a crowded channel, or a device that has been accidentally switched to a weak band. Almost every one of them is fixable without buying new gear.

This guide covers the fixes in order, from the free work (placement, band choice, channel, interference) through the hardware upgrades that actually change things (new router, mesh system, wireless extender, powerline) and ties each to what it fixes. The point is not to spend money on the widest possible thing; it is to spend on what fixes your specific problem.

You need your router, about 30 minutes to diagnose and fix the free stuff, and a phone with a Wi-Fi signal app if you want to map your house properly - optional but helpful. No special expertise required.

## Step 1: Map the Problem

Before buying anything, work out what "bad" actually looks like. Walk through the house with a Wi-Fi scanner app (Android: **WiFiman** or **WiFi Analyzer**; iOS: the built-in Field Test in Settings or a reputable scanner app) and note signal at each room, ideally standing where you use devices.

Write down for each spot:

- Signal strength (a rough number - most people use -50 dBm to -60 dBm as "good", -65 to -70 as marginal, worse than -75 as "poor")
- Which band you are on (2.4 GHz or 5 GHz)
- Whether the speed feels fine or stalls on video calls and large transfers

Do this at the times your Wi-Fi is worst (evening is a classic - neighbours all come home). What you need to fix depends entirely on where the problem is. A single dead corner is a different fix than a whole house that is thin.

If your map shows the problem is near a specific wall, appliance or window, that points to a physical blocking issue, which you fix with placement and path, not money.

## Step 2: Move the Router to Its Best Local Spot

The single biggest speedup for many homes is moving the router. The best spot is:

- **Central to the house's use**, not central to the building's floorspace whole. Put it near where life happens, not mathematically in the middle of the house but far from bedrooms.
- **Elevated and out in the open** - on a shelf, not under a table; not buried inside a cabinet or behind a television. Many people put routers out of sight, and Wi-Fi hates too much of the same surrounding material.
- **Away from major obstacles** - metal filing cabinets, large appliances, heaters, fish tanks (water blocks signal), mirrors. Avoid stacking directly against drywall with no breathing room.
- **With clear line-of-sight towards the worst room if possible** - the fewer walls and floors between router and use area, the better.

If the router is currently dead in a corner cupboard under the desk, moving it to a shelf in an open spot often does more than any extender.

That is a small experiment you can run in minutes: move the router, run your map again, compare. If it is worse, move it back.

## Step 3: Pick the Right Band per Device

Dual-band routers broadcast 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. Each has a different strength:

- **5 GHz** - less saturated, faster for the same distance, but shorter range and worse at penetrating walls. Best for close devices, video calls and gaming.
- **2.4 GHz** - better range, better through walls, but often crowded with neighbours and more prone to interference. Best for further rooms, smart home devices and basic browsing.

Many routers hide both under one name and "steer" devices; steering is not always good. For the best experience, give them different names if your router allows - `home-5G` and `home-2.4` - so you can connect each device to the band that suits it. Video calls and streaming go to 5 GHz wherever they are reasonably close; general phone browsing at range uses 2.4 GHz.

Note that the router's "smart connect" feature can be the source of odd behaviour where devices stubbornly stick to a crowded 2.4 GHz channel when 5 GHz is available nearby. Don't assume steering is always right; test both.

## Step 4: Tame 2.4 GHz Interference

2.4 GHz Wi-Fi shares its air with several other things. The biggest are:

- **Microwaves** - when running, they can kill 2.4 GHz heavily for the few seconds they are on. If your kitchen is your poor spot and the router is near the microwave, the router and the microwave should not be near each other.
- **Bluetooth devices** - they share the band; a crowded Bluetooth environment near the router can add noise.
- **Neighbour Wi-Fi** - when everyone parks on channel 1, 6 or 11, the band gets busy. If you cannot move the router far from neighbours, fight the channel.

Reducing interference: move the router away from the microwave if you have one in the same room, keep other RF sources clear, and when the 2.4 GHz band feels cramped at commute-home times, move video calls and streaming explicitly to 5 GHz rather than hoping steering will.

## Step 5: Choose a Better Channel

On 2.4 GHz, the bands are divided into channels 1 through 11 (in most regions). Channels overlap, and channels 1, 6 and 11 do not overlap each other - that is why routers often default to one of those three. If neighbouring networks cluster on channels 1 and 11, using channel 6 cleanly can feel faster.

Scan with a Wi-Fi analyzer app and look at a channel map of nearby networks. If yours is crowded with others on the same channel, change to a quieter one. On 5 GHz, there are many more, non-overlapping channels, so a scan is usually less necessary unless the area is very congested and 5 GHz itself is crowded.

To change channels, log into the router's admin page (see our [home Wi-Fi guide](/posts/how-to-secure-your-home-wifi-network/) for how to reach it and find wireless settings) and set the 2.4 GHz channel manually to the quietest non-overlapping option you see. Set 5 GHz similarly if there is obvious crowding there.

If the router already has an "auto" channel selection and it is not bad, you may not need to touch it. But auto does not always keep up in dense apartments; manual is a simple win there.

## Step 6: Try Better Antennas or Restart Routinely

- **External antennas:** if your router has detachable antennas and you want a directional pattern toward a particular room, change antenna orientation (horizontal versus vertical) to aim more energy that way. Some routers ship with weak stock antennas that a higher-gain replacement can help if the other fixes are done first and you still have a weak spot at the far end.
- **Reboots help:** routers are small computers that drift over time. A restart clears stale caches and re-establishes connections. If your Wi-Fi is fine most of the week and only drifts worse on weekends, a weekly restart (once a week) can recover lost performance for free. Many people restart only when a problem appears; doing it on a schedule prevents the moment that triggers the problem.

## Step 7: Understand the Hardware Upgrade Ladder

If the free fixes are not enough, these are your real options, in order of how much they change things:

**Upgrade the router.** If yours is old, slow, only 2.4 GHz capable, or has a weak radio, a modern router with Wi-Fi 5 or Wi-Fi 6, better internal antennas and multiple radios can move the whole house up a notch. Worth it if the old router is genuinely old or only one band.

**Mesh Wi-Fi system.** Several nodes that work together as one network, with seamless roaming. They are built for whole-home coverage and tend to replace extending networks rather than complement them. Good when a single router cannot cover a large or oddly shaped home, or when you want a single consistent network name and no thinking about bands. More expensive than a single router but far simpler to live with in big homes.

**Wireless range extender / repeater.** Extends the signal from your router to a further area. Simpler and cheaper, but as noted, it usually halves bandwidth to the extended segment for devices on it, and you may end up with a second network name you have to connect to manually unless the device supports seamless roaming. Best as a targeted fix for one dead corner for casual use, not as a whole-house strategy.

**Powerline adapters.** Use the home's electrical wiring to send internet to another room, then broadcast Wi-Fi or Ethernet there. Effective when walls are thick and placing a router or mesh node closer is impractical; not great if the wiring is poor or the sending and receiving points are on different electrical circuits. Good alternative to wireless extenders in some houses, bad in others.

**Ethernet backhaul to a Wi-Fi access point.** Running a cable (or using powerline) to a room and putting a real access point there gives strong local Wi-Fi in that room without repeating a weak signal. The most reliable fix for a single large dead zone - but it needs a cable run.

## Step 8: Install a Mesh System the Right Way

If you go mesh, placement matters hugely; the nodes have to be close enough that they can talk to each other well.

- **Main node near the modem/router position**, ideally central to the house, connected well.
- **Satellite nodes** placed roughly halfway between the main node and the dead rooms, not all the way out in the dead rooms alone. A node dead in a corner with bad upstream reception is itself a weak source.
- **Test each node's backhaul connection** via its app. If a node reports poor connection to the main node, move it closer. This is the most common mesh mistake - putting nodes at the dead spot rather than at the point where they can hear the upstream well.
- **Use wired backhaul** if you can run a cable between a couple of nodes - gives the best of both worlds (strong main radio, strong local node) and avoids the wireless backhaul drop.

## Step 9: One Network or Separate?

Complex setups with repeater networks, two SSIDs and older devices can lead to devices choosing the weak band without you noticing. Simpler is often better:

- One strong mesh network with good coverage everywhere is the best experience for most homes and most people.
- If you must run extenders, make them seamless if possible so devices roam; if not seamless, name them distinctly so you can connect a phone to the strong one on purpose.

The best network is the one that stays out of your head. If you are thinking about which network to join, something needs to change.

## Step 10: Prove the Fix

After making a change - router move, band choice, channel, new hardware - rerun your Wi-Fi map, at the time of day the problem is worst, and compare side by side. One number (signal dBm) plus one real experience (can the video call in that room survive?) is the only proof that matters. If an extender fixed the back room's signal to -60 from -80 and the same video call now does not drop, that is the fix. If a mesh node only reports poor backhaul but looks "connected," move it before resigning it to life.

If the fix is supposed to help a room and the number did not move, the change is not doing what you hoped - either placement is wrong (move the device), or the hardware is not what the room needed (try wired powerline, or a better node), or the root cause is not Wi-Fi at all (maybe the device is bad, the Ethernet in that room is bad, the problem is actually the device, not the network). Measure before you decide what to buy next.

## When the Problem Is Not Wi-Fi

Sometimes the weak network is actually a weak device or a software issue:

- An old phone with a weak antenna genuinely performs worse than a modern one at the same signal, even if the site looks fine from a laptop.
- A device on a crowded network and doing a background download can make everything else feel slow even if the Wi-Fi signal is good. Check what your devices are doing.
- Interference from inside the same device (a phone case with metal blocking the antenna, a laptop Wi-Fi card blocked by its body on a metal surface) can make one room feel bad when the room's Wi-Fi is fine.
- A slow internet connection overall masquerades as Wi-Fi problems. Run a wired speed test near the router with all other devices off; if the wired connection itself is slow, the issue is your ISP, not the house.

Check the wired baseline first if you suspect it is not Wi-Fi - it saves money misdirected at repeaters and mesh systems.

## Troubleshooting the Common Complaints

**"Wi-Fi is fine in one room, flat-out terrible in another."** Likely a physical blockage (wall, floor, appliance) and a band mismatch - move the router towards that room's side, use 2.4 GHz there, or place a mesh satellite between them.

**"Everything is slow even though signal looks okay."** 2.4 GHz congestion, neighbour interference, or an old router on an old standard. Scan for crowding, move video/heavy traffic to 5 GHz, and check the baseline wired speed.

**"It worked fine and now it does not."** Often interference that changed (daytime versus evening, rainy versus dry because wet walls and foliage change propagation a little, new microwave, new neighbour's router), or a device that changed channels / band settings. Re-establish the map, and check the router status page.

**"Video calls keep dropping."** Usually the room's signal near the caller is marginal *and* the retrying eats the call. For calls, prefer 5 GHz close, or move the caller to the better-spot room for the call, or use the wired connection option if available (Ethernet is worth it for one permanently placed work station in a bad room).

**"My smart home devices keep disconnecting."** Smart devices usually run on 2.4 GHz and are sensitive to band and channel crowding and to being far from the router. Give them a stable 2.4 GHz connection in a good spot, maybe a dedicated smart-device network if your router supports it.

## Security Review for a Bigger or Different Setup

Adding nodes, extenders and a new router does not change the fundamentals from our [home Wi-Fi security guide](/posts/how-to-secure-your-home-wifi-network/): a real password, WPA3 or WPA2/WPA3 mixed where possible, updated firmware, a guest network for visitors and IoT, and a periodic audit. In fact, a new or bigger system is the moment to set those up properly instead of inheriting the old weak defaults again.

## The Bottom Line

The right Wi-Fi fix depends on whether the issue is placement, band, interference, a weak old router, or a house that genuinely needs more radios. Start with the free work - put the router where it can breathe and reach the worst room, use 5 GHz for close work and 2.4 GHz for range, reduce crowding, maybe pick a cleaner channel - and then, if needed, move up the hardware ladder from new router to mesh (best for whole-home) to a targeted extender or powerline for one problematic corner. Prove each change with a before-and-after map at the time of day the problem is worst. Most homes do not need the most expensive option; they need the right option, and the right one is almost always obvious after you map the problem rather than after someone tells you what to buy.
