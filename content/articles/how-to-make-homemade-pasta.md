Homemade pasta has a reputation for being fiddly, but the basics are simple: flour, eggs, a little care, and a willingness to work the dough until it comes together. A good fresh pasta is tender, silky, and satisfying in a way that dried pasta only sometimes matches. It is also one of those kitchen projects that feels more impressive than it is difficult, once you understand the feel of the dough.

This guide walks through making fresh pasta from scratch in a simple, reliable way. It covers the basic ingredients, mixing, kneading, resting, rolling, cutting, and cooking, along with the little mistakes that can make pasta tough, sticky, or uneven. It is written for the home cook who wants real fresh pasta without needing a long list of equipment or a specialised pantry.

You need flour, eggs, a little salt, a surface, a rolling pin or pasta machine if you have one, a knife or cutter for shaping, and a pot for boiling. That is enough for a lot of pasta. If you have a machine, great; if not, you can still make excellent pasta by hand.

## The Basic Ingredients

For a simple egg pasta, the classic ingredients are flour, eggs, and salt. Some recipes add a little oil, but the basic dough does not need much beyond flour, eggs, and salt.

- **Flour.** Plain flour works well for a simple pasta. Tipo 00 flour is a common choice for fresh pasta because it is finely milled and can make a silky dough, but it is not the only option. If you use plain flour, the pasta may be a little less delicate, but it can still be excellent.
- **Eggs.** Eggs are what make this a fresh egg pasta. They add richness, structure, and flavour. The usual starting point is to use eggs with flour in a ratio that gives a workable dough; the exact ratio depends on the recipe and the flour, but the idea is to get a dough that is firm but pliable.
- **Salt.** A little salt improves flavour. It is not a huge part of the dough, but it matters.

You do not need many ingredients. The simplicity is part of the appeal. Once you can make a basic dough, you can use it for a lot of shapes and dishes.

## Mixing the Dough

Start by putting the flour on a clean surface or in a bowl. Make a well in the centre if you are working on the surface, or simply combine in a bowl. Add the eggs and salt, then mix until the dough comes together.

At first, the dough may look a little rough or uneven. That is normal. Keep working it until the ingredients are fully combined and the dough forms a coherent ball. If it is very dry and crumbly, add a little water; if it is too sticky, add a little flour. Do this slowly and in small amounts, because it is easy to overcorrect.

The goal at this stage is not perfection. It is a dough that comes together and is ready to knead.

## Kneading

Kneading develops the dough so it becomes smooth, elastic, and workable. For pasta, you want the dough firm enough to roll well, but not so stiff that it fights you.

Knead by hand on a clean surface. Push the dough away from you with the heel of your hand, fold it back, give it a small turn, and repeat. Keep going until the dough is smooth and feels elastic. This can take a little while, especially if the dough is new to you, but it is not endless.

The dough is ready when it is smooth and feels uniform, with a little spring to it. It should not be sticky or crumbly. It should be firm enough to roll without fighting you, but soft enough to shape.

If the dough is too dry, add a very small amount of water and knead it in. If it is too sticky, add a little flour. Make small adjustments; pasta dough is more forgiving than bread dough in some ways, but it still likes the right balance.

## Resting

After kneading, let the dough rest. This is important. Resting relaxes the gluten and makes the dough easier to roll.

Wrap the dough in cling film or cover it with a bowl or cloth, and let it sit for a while. Even a short rest helps, but a longer rest is often better. If you have time, give it half an hour or more. The dough will be easier to roll and less likely to spring back.

Do not skip the rest if you can help it. Pasta dough that is rested rolls more evenly and is less likely to fight you when you flatten it.

## Rolling the Pasta

Once rested, roll the dough out. You can use a rolling pin or a pasta machine.

If you use a rolling pin, work from the centre outward, turning the dough as you go, and try to keep the sheet an even thickness. It takes a little patience. Roll, turn, roll, turn. The dough should get thinner and smoother.

If you use a pasta machine, pass the dough through the widest setting first, then fold or repeat as needed, then move to thinner settings gradually. Do not rush from thick to thin in one jump; the dough is easier to handle if you work through the settings.

The thickness you want depends on what you are making. Some pasta is best a little thicker; some is better very thin. For most fresh pasta, you want it thin enough to cook quickly and feel tender, but not so thin that it tears. A sheet you can almost see through is a common standard for many shapes.

If the dough sticks while rolling, a little flour can help, but use it sparingly. Too much flour on the sheet can make the pasta dry or dusty.

## Cutting the Pasta

Once the sheet is rolled, cut it into shapes. The shape depends on what you want.

- **Ribbons.** Roll the sheet up loosely and cut strips of the width you want, then unfurl them. This works for fettuccine, tagliatelle, and similar shapes.
- **Small shapes.** You can cut squares, diamonds, or other shapes for filled or shaped pasta.
- **Filled pasta.** If you want ravioli or similar, you need a filling and a way to seal it. That is a little more work, but it uses the same basic dough.
- **Hand-cut or machine-cut.** A knife can cut many shapes; a machine can make long, even strands more easily.

After cutting, the pasta is usually left to dry a little if you are not cooking it immediately, or it can be cooked right away. Fresh pasta can be cooked soon after cutting, but if you let it sit, it may need a little more time and care.

## Cooking Fresh Pasta

Fresh pasta cooks much faster than dried pasta, so be ready for that. It often cooks in a minute or two, sometimes less, depending on thickness.

Use plenty of boiling salted water. Salt the water well; pasta tastes better when the water is properly salted. Drop the pasta in, stir it gently so it does not stick together, and watch it carefully.

Fresh pasta is done when it is tender but still has a little bite. It often floats, or looks ready by texture and colour. Taste it. That is the best test. Do not leave it in the water too long, because fresh pasta can go from done to overcooked quickly.

Drain it well, and serve it with the sauce you plan to use. Fresh pasta is often best with sauces that cling to it and complement its texture, rather than a heavily watery sauce that drowns it.

## Simple Sauces to Use With Fresh Pasta

Fresh pasta is good with a wide range of sauces, but some work especially well.

- **Butter and cheese.** A simple butter sauce with cheese is a classic for fresh pasta. It is quick and lets the pasta shine.
- **Tomato-based sauces.** A good tomato sauce works well with fresh pasta, especially if the pasta is a shape that holds sauce.
- **Oil-based sauces.** Olive oil with garlic, herbs, or vegetables can be a good partner.
- **Creamy sauces.** Fresh pasta can work well with cream-based sauces, though it is often good to keep them balanced so the pasta still feels fresh and not overly heavy.

The best sauce is one that suits the pasta shape and the dough. Fresh pasta is versatile, but it tends to shine when the sauce is good without overwhelming it.

## Common Mistakes

A few mistakes come up often when making fresh pasta.

- **Dough too dry.** This makes the pasta hard to roll and can make it tough. Add a little water if needed.
- **Dough too wet.** This makes it sticky and harder to handle. Add a little flour, but not too much.
- **Not kneading enough.** The dough may be uneven or not hold together as well.
- **Not resting enough.** The dough may fight you when rolling and shrink back.
- **Rolling too thick.** The pasta may take too long to cook and may feel heavy.
- **Overcooking.** Fresh pasta cooks fast; leave it in too long and it can get soft or mushy.
- **Not salting the water.** Pasta tastes better in properly salted water.

Most of these are easy to avoid once you know what to look for. Pasta dough is forgiving, but it rewards attention.

## A Few Practical Tips

- Work on a clean, smooth surface.
- Keep the dough covered when you are not working it, so it does not dry out.
- If the dough fights you while rolling, let it rest a little longer.
- Cut the pasta close to when you plan to cook it, if possible.
- Salt the water well; it makes a real difference.
- Taste for doneness instead of relying only on time.
- Serve soon after cooking if you can.

Fresh pasta is not difficult once you have made it a few times. The first one may feel slow, but after a while the rhythm becomes familiar.

## The Bottom Line

Make homemade pasta by mixing flour, eggs, and salt into a smooth, firm dough, kneading it until it is elastic, resting it, rolling it thin, cutting it into shapes, and cooking it quickly in well-salted boiling water. The ingredients are simple, the method is straightforward, and the result is worth it. Fresh pasta is tender and silky, and it rewards a little care at each step. Once you have made it, it stops feeling like a special project and starts feeling like something you can actually do.
