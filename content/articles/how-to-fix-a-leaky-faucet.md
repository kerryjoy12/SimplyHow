A leaky faucet is one of those small household problems that is easy to ignore until you notice the sound, the wasted water, or the slow stain it leaves. A drip may seem harmless, but a faucet that drips all day wastes a surprising amount of water and can wear grooves into a sink, and the constant tick of a drip is enough to make anyone want it fixed. The good news is that most faucet leaks are simple enough to fix in a few minutes once you know what part is failing and have the right replacement.

This guide walks through the whole process: shutting the water off safely, figuring out what kind of faucet you have, identifying the part that is leaking, replacing the washer, cartridge, O-ring, or valve seat as needed, and putting everything back together without losing the small parts or making it worse. It also covers the common mistakes that turn a simple repair into a larger one, and when replacing the whole faucet is the better choice.

You should be able to follow it with basic tools - an adjustable wrench or a set of wrenches, a screwdriver, a few plugs or rag, and a container for parts - plus the replacement parts matched to your faucet. For a first repair, patience and careful disassembly matter more than special skill.

## Find the Leak Before You Start

Not every leak is inside the faucet. Before taking anything apart, look at where the water is actually going.

- **Dripping from the spout** usually points to a washer, cartridge, O-ring, or valve seat inside the faucet.
- **Leaking around the base** of the faucet can mean a worn O-ring, a loose connection, or a problem where the faucet meets the sink.
- **Leaking underneath the sink** may be a supply line, a shut-off valve, or a connection rather than the faucet itself.
- **Leaking only when the faucet is on** can be a different problem than a faucet that drips when shut off.

This matters because the fix for a spout drip is not always the fix for a base leak or a supply-line leak. The goal of the diagnosis is to avoid replacing parts that are not the problem.

If the leak is from under the sink or the supply line, the faucet repair in this guide may not be what you need. Check the visible supply lines and valves first if water is pooling under the sink.

## Shut Off the Water

Before you disassemble anything, shut off the water.

- Shut off the supply valves under the sink if they are accessible and work.
- If there are no working under-sink valves, shut off the main water supply to the house.
- Open the faucet after shutting off the water to drain what remains and to confirm the water is off.
- If the faucet still runs after you shut the valve, the valve itself may be the problem, and you may need to work around that or shut off the main.

It is a good habit to plug the drain before you start. Small screws, washers, and springs have a way of disappearing down a drain the moment you drop them.

Lay a towel on the sink or countertop, and set a container for parts. A quick photo before disassembly can save you during reassembly, especially on a faucet style you have not seen before.

## Identify the Faucet Type

Different faucets fail in different ways, and the repair depends on the type. The main styles you will meet are:

- **Compression faucets.** These have separate hot and cold handles and work by compressing a washer against a valve seat. A worn washer is the classic compression-faucet leak.
- **Cartridge faucets.** These use a cartridge that controls the flow. Drips often come from a worn cartridge or seals inside it.
- **Ball faucets.** These have a rounded ball inside that controls water flow. Leaks can come from worn seals, springs, or the ball itself.
- **Disk faucets.** These use a ceramic disk assembly. They tend to be durable, but they can leak if the disks or seals fail.

You may not need to know all the internal details, but you do need to know which type you are working on so you can get the right replacement part. The brand and model help, but the old part itself, once removed, is often the best reference.

If you are not sure what you have, disassemble only as far as needed to see the internal parts, and then decide what to replace. Do not assume all faucets use the same washer or cartridge.

## Gather Tools and Parts

You will usually need:

- A way to shut off the water
- An adjustable wrench or wrenches, or a socket set as needed
- A screwdriver, often a flat-head or Phillips depending on the faucet
- Allen keys if your faucet uses them for set screws
- Pliers, sometimes, for stubborn parts
- Replacement parts matched to the faucet: washer, cartridge, O-ring, seat, or springs as needed
- A rag, towel, or plug for the sink
- A small container for screws and parts

If possible, get the replacement part before you take the faucet apart, or be prepared to carry the old part to the store to match it. Faucet parts are not always interchangeable across brands and models.

## Fix a Compression Faucet

A compression faucet is one of the simpler older styles. It has separate hot and cold handles, and each handle presses a stem down onto a valve seat. The washer at the bottom of the stem is the part that most often wears out and causes a drip.

### Disassemble

1. Shut off the water and open the faucet to drain it.
2. Remove the handle. There is usually a cap or cover that hides a screw; pry it off carefully and remove the screw.
3. Lift the handle off. If it is stuck, tap it gently or wiggle it; do not force it with brute force.
4. Under the handle, remove the packing nut or stem assembly.
5. Remove the stem and look at the washer at the bottom.

### Find the worn part

On a compression faucet, the washer is the most common leak source. If it is worn, cracked, or flattened, it will not seal well. Also check the valve seat inside the faucet body. If the seat is pitted or corroded, a new washer may not seal well until the seat is serviced or replaced.

### Replace the washer, O-ring, and seat as needed

- Replace the washer with one that matches the old one in size and shape.
- Replace the O-ring or packing if it looks worn.
- If the valve seat is damaged, use a seat dressing tool if the seat is serviceable, or replace the seat if possible. On some faucets, a badly corroded seat is a reason to replace the faucet.

A common approach is to replace the washer, O-ring, and any small parts that look worn at the same time, since they are inexpensive compared with the hassle of a second repair soon after.

### Reassemble

Put the stem back in, reattach the handle, and tighten everything to snug, not to the maximum. Turn the water back on slowly and test the faucet. If it still drips, recheck the washer, the seat, and the alignment.

## Fix a Cartridge Faucet

Cartridge faucets are common in both single-handle and two-handle designs. They often leak because the cartridge or its seals wear out.

### Disassemble

1. Shut off the water and open the faucet to drain it.
2. Remove the handle, which may be held by a set screw or a cap.
3. Remove any decorative cover, trim, or retainer that holds the cartridge in place.
4. Pull the cartridge out. On some faucets it comes out easily; on others it may be stuck and need gentle persuasion.

### Match the cartridge

Cartridges are not all the same. A cartridge that fits one faucet will not necessarily fit another. Match the replacement to the old cartridge, by brand and shape and the way it seats. If you are unsure, take the old cartridge with you or look up the faucet model carefully.

### Replace the cartridge and seals

- Install the new cartridge in the same orientation as the old one.
- Replace any O-rings, seals, or springs if they are worn.
- Clean the housing before reinstalling the cartridge.
- Reassemble the faucet, turn the water back on, and test.

If the faucet still leaks after cartridge replacement, double-check the installation, the seals, and the housing for damage.

## Fix a Ball Faucet

A ball faucet has a rotating ball inside that controls flow. Leaks can come from the ball, the seals, or the springs.

### Disassemble

1. Shut off the water and drain the faucet.
2. Remove the handle, often held by a set screw under a cap.
3. Remove the cap and the ball assembly.
4. Look at the seals and springs inside the faucet body.

### Replace seals and springs

On many ball faucets, the leak is from worn rubber seals or springs rather than the ball itself. Replace the seals and springs in the kit if you have one, or match them to the old parts.

### Reassemble

Put the ball back in the correct orientation, reinstall the seals and springs, and reassemble the handle and trim. Turn the water on and test. If the drip continues, check whether the ball is scratched or the seat under it is damaged.

## Fix a Disk Faucet

Disk faucets use a ceramic disk assembly. They are usually reliable, but leaks can still happen if the disks are dirty, misaligned, or worn.

### Disassemble

1. Shut off the water and drain the faucet.
2. Remove the handle and any trim.
3. Remove the disk assembly or the parts holding it.

### Inspect and replace

- Clean the disks if they are dirty.
- Replace the cartridge or seal kit if the disks or seals are worn.
- Reassemble carefully and test.

Disk faucets often need less frequent repair than compression faucets, but when they do leak, a seal or cartridge replacement is usually the fix.

## Fix a Base Leak

A leak around the base of the faucet can come from a worn O-ring, a loose connection, or a problem where the faucet meets the sink.

- If the leak is around the base and the faucet is a type with an O-ring at the base, replacing that O-ring can help.
- If the faucet is loose, tightening the mounting nuts under the sink may stop some leaks.
- If the leak is from the supply line or the shut-off valve under the sink, the faucet repair itself may not be the right fix.

If you cannot tell whether the leak is from the faucet body or from a supply line, dry the area and watch carefully where new water appears.

## Reassembly and Testing

Reassembly is where many repairs go wrong, not because the part was wrong but because something was missed, misarranged, or over-tightened.

- Make sure every part goes back in the right orientation.
- Do not over-tighten screws or nuts. Snug is usually enough; excessive force can damage the faucet or strip threads.
- Check that the handle moves correctly and the faucet opens and shuts as it should.
- Turn the water back on slowly and test for leaks at the spout and around the base.
- If the faucet drips, recheck the washer, cartridge, O-ring, or seat before assuming the repair failed.

A careful reassembly often matters as much as the replacement itself.

## When to Replace the Whole Faucet

Sometimes replacing the whole faucet is the better choice.

- The faucet is old and the parts are hard to find.
- The body is corroded, cracked, or damaged.
- The valve seat is badly damaged and cannot be serviced.
- You have replaced the parts and the leak continues.
- You want a more modern faucet that is easier to use or more water-efficient.

A new faucet can solve a stubborn leak and improve the sink area at the same time. If the repair keeps failing, replacement is often cheaper and less frustrating than repeated attempts on a failing faucet.

## Common Mistakes

- Not shutting off the water before disassembly.
- Losing small parts down the drain.
- Forgetting to match the replacement part correctly.
- Over-tightening screws and stripping threads.
- Missing a worn valve seat on a compression faucet.
- Confusing the faucet type and buying the wrong part.
- Reassembly in the wrong order.
- Ignoring a supply-line leak and thinking it is the faucet.

The most expensive faucet repair is often the one you have to do twice because of a simple mistake.

## A Simple Repair Plan

For most leaky faucets, this plan works:

1. Diagnose where the leak is.
2. Shut off the water and drain the faucet.
3. Plug the drain and take photos.
4. Remove the handle and the internal parts.
5. Identify the worn part.
6. Replace the washer, cartridge, O-ring, or seat as needed.
7. Reassemble carefully.
8. Test for leaks.

That is usually enough for a drip that was caused by a worn part.

## A Final Note on Water Waste

A dripping faucet is not only annoying; it wastes water. Fixing it is one of the small household tasks that pays for itself quickly. If the repair is a simple washer or cartridge swap, the cost is low and the benefit is immediate. If the faucet is beyond repair, replacing it is a practical way to stop the waste and improve the sink.

## The Bottom Line

Fix a leaky faucet by shutting off the water, identifying the faucet type, removing the worn part, and replacing it with the matching washer, cartridge, O-ring, or seat. Reassemble carefully, do not over-tighten, and test for leaks. If the faucet is old, damaged, or still leaking after repair, replace it. Most drips are a small, affordable repair; the key is matching the part and taking the time to do it right.
